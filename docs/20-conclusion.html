Conclusion: where the analysis points

What the diagnostics said

The recursive bivariate probit returned a statistically significant protective effect of C-section on under-24-month mortality in 2022 Bangladesh (−1.51 percentage points, cluster-bootstrap 95% CI [−2.83, −0.19] pp). The diagnostic chain in Part II rejected the identifying assumptions on which that estimate rested:

Chapter Diagnostic Verdict
8 Wooldridge endogeneity test (2SRI residual) Cannot reject exogeneity for C-section in any wave (p > 0.19 in all four)
9 Cross-instrument placebo 8/8 cells reject exclusion at p < 10⁻⁷
10 Copula sensitivity 5/8 ATEs flip sign across Gaussian/Frank/Clayton
11 Conley plausibly-exogenous bounds LPM-2SLS CI spans zero in all 4 waves at γ = 0
12 E-value CI-based E-value 1.25 — confounders of RR ≥ 1.25 nullify
13 Cinelli-Hazlett RV 7.2% (4.6% at α=0.05) — confounder partial R² of 5% nullifies
14 Acerenza testable inequalities Inequality #5 rejected; one or more identifying assumptions violated
15 Predetermined Chow at 2011 F = 246, p = 0.004 — regime break structurally detectable

The estimator works. The identification doesn’t.

The methodological pivot

The brief’s central argument has three parts.

First — the IV problem is structural, not implementation. The community-LOO IV used by the RBVP cannot satisfy the exclusion restriction in this setting. This is Angrist (Angrist 2014) and Betz, Cook & Hollenbach (Betz et al. 2018) applied to Bangladesh DHS data. The chapter 9 placebo test is unambiguous evidence; no amount of cleaner code or better controls fixes it.

Second — the outcome is wrong for the new regime. Mortality fell from ~108 to ~37 per 1,000 live births across the observation window for reasons that are not about C-section access — nutrition, sanitation, vaccine coverage. C-section’s marginal contribution to mortality in a demand-driven, low-baseline-risk population is small and easily masked. The relevant outcomes for elective C-section in a regime where supply is no longer the binding constraint are morbidity outcomes: microbiome composition, asthma and allergic disease (Kleij et al. 2024), neurodevelopment, type-1 diabetes incidence. These are tracked in the recent literature and not measurable from cross-sectional BDHS waves.

Third — the design is wrong for the new identification problem. Community-level and spatial instruments (Angrist 2014; Betz et al. 2018) cannot recover causal effects in regime-shifted populations. The alternative design, well established in the healthcare-economics literature, is provider-level IV: variation across hospitals or obstetricians in their propensity to recommend C-section, conditional on observed patient characteristics (Doyle et al. 2015; Chyn et al. 2024). Card-Dobkin-Maestas (Card et al. 2009) use a similar logic at the policy level for Medicare. None of these designs use community-level IVs. All of them require administrative data linkable to outcomes — data that BDHS does not provide.

What this study contributes

This book is the methodological audit, not a positive causal estimate. Its contributions:

  1. A worked example of building a sophisticated IV estimator and then running its own diagnostic chain to conclude the identification is broken. This is the methodological move the original PLOS One and SSM-Population Health submissions did not make.

  2. A reproducible pipeline. Fourteen documented bug fixes (A1–A14) brought the original analysis into reproducible form. The pipeline graph, methodology map, and full changelog are in the appendices.

  3. A concrete pivot direction. Future work should: (a) move to morbidity outcomes (microbiome, asthma, neurodevelopment), (b) use provider-level rather than spatial instruments (Doyle et al. 2015; Chyn et al. 2024), (c) link DHS-style cross-sectional cohorts to administrative birth and hospital records, (d) pre-register the design before looking at the data (Nosek et al. 2022; Hernán and Robins 2016).

  4. Multi-country generalization. The Pakistan and India robustness chapters show the diagnostic failures are not a Bangladesh peculiarity. The full South Asian DHS/NFHS panel exhibits the same regime-shift identification problem.

What’s deliberately not done

This book does not include:

  • A structural equation model of latent maternal-care quality.
  • A Bayesian spatiotemporal model (e.g., Stan or INLA).
  • A formal COVID-19 disruption analysis with 2020-era data.

Each of these is a natural extension. None changes the diagnostic conclusion in this volume: a community-level instrument cannot identify the question that was asked.
