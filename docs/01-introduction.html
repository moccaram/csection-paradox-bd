Introduction: a wave-by-wave sign flip

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The puzzle

A standard wave-by-wave logistic regression on Bangladesh DHS data spanning 2004–2022 (N = 33,732 children, 62 candidate predictors) returns a result that shouldn’t be possible if the underlying causal mechanism is stable: the C-section coefficient changes sign across the eighteen-year observation window.

In 2004, C-section is associated with higher child mortality. In 2022, C-section is associated with lower child mortality.

Two coefficients with opposite signs from the same descriptive model on overlapping populations is not noise. It is a structural finding — large enough to rule out sampling variance, structured enough to demand a causal investigation.

This book documents that investigation. A recursive bivariate probit (RBVP) estimator (Marra and Radice 2011; Greene 2012) is built and run; three formal identification diagnostics (Swanson and Hernán 2013; Terza 2018; McGovern et al. 2015) are applied; a modern sensitivity layer (VanderWeele and Ding 2017; Cinelli and Hazlett 2020; Acerenza et al. 2023) tests the assumptions on which the headline estimate rests. The conclusion of the diagnostic chain is not “C-section reduces mortality.” The conclusion is that the identifying assumptions on which a causal interpretation depends are rejected by the data.

The substantive backdrop

The reason the sign flips, the book argues, is that the data-generating process underlying C-section selection changed dramatically between 2004 and 2022:

  • 2004: C-section rate ≈ 4.5%. Surgery is access-constrained; women who reach a facility tend to be high-risk emergencies. Selection on severity dominates.
  • 2022: C-section rate ≈ 44%, well past the WHO 15% threshold (Organization 2015; Sujon et al. 2025). Surgery is demand-driven, increasingly elective, concentrated in private facilities and upper wealth quintiles.

This is a regime transition. Standard IV strategies that assume a fixed selection mechanism cannot identify a single coherent treatment effect across that span. Chapters 6–11 work out the formal consequences; chapter 19 extends the analysis to India and Pakistan; Chapter 20 names the methodological pivot the data implies.

Three reader paths through this book

For social scientists, the salient chapters are Part I (descriptive trajectory, regime transition, wealth gradient, mortality trends) and the modern-inference layer in Chapter 15 (predetermined Chow test, the regime-shift’s structural visibility). For IV econometricians, Chapter 6 (the econometric framework), Chapter 9 (cross-instrument placebo), and Chapter 11 (Conley plausibly-exogenous bounds) carry the methodological argument. For statisticians, Chapter 7 (RBVP), Chapter 8 (2SRI Hausman test), Chapter 10 (copula sensitivity), and Chapter 14 (Acerenza testable inequalities) interrogate the bivariate probit’s identifying assumptions.

The conclusion (Chapter 20) is written for all three audiences in turn.
